The fundamentals

What house hacking actually is

House hacking means buying a property with more than one unit, living in one of them, and renting out the rest. The rent from your tenants goes toward your mortgage instead of into a landlord's pocket, so your own housing cost drops, sometimes down to zero, sometimes into positive cash flow.

It is not a loophole and it is not new. Investors have done this for decades with duplexes, triplexes, and fourplexes. What makes it work for veterans specifically is the VA loan, which treats a two to four unit property the same way it treats a single family home, as long as you live in one of the units.

The honest version, not the Instagram version: it usually does not hand you instant passive income. The realistic outcome, backed by real numbers rather than a highlight reel, is that your tenants cover most or all of your mortgage while you live there, and your housing cost goes from a few thousand dollars a month to close to nothing. That alone is the win, the cash flow on top of that is a bonus, not the baseline.

The VA loan side

How a VA loan actually works on a two to four unit property

With full entitlement, a VA loan still allows zero percent down on a multifamily purchase, the same as it would on a single family home. There is no VA set loan cap at zero down, your approved amount comes down to what the lender qualifies you for and what the property appraises at.

You have to certify that you will move into one of the units within 60 days of closing and live there as your primary residence. This cannot be a pure rental purchase, the VA loan exists for you to live there, the other units are a bonus, not the point on paper.

Here is the part most people do not know going in: lenders typically only count 75 percent of the rent from your other units toward qualifying you for the loan, not the full amount. To use that rental income at all, you generally need either a documented history as a landlord or a property management company lined up, plus six months of mortgage, tax, and insurance payments sitting in reserve. Every unit, including any that are empty, also has to pass VA property condition standards at appraisal, not just the one you plan to live in.

None of this is a reason to avoid the strategy, it is just the real mechanics, and knowing them before your first conversation with a lender saves you a round of surprises.

Running the math

What the numbers actually look like

Take a $349,000 fourplex. Zero down means your full purchase price becomes your loan amount. At a 6.25 percent rate over 30 years, principal and interest alone runs a little over $2,100 a month. Add property tax and insurance and you are near $2,600 before anything else.

Now bring in the other three units. If each rents for $1,250, that is $3,750 a month in potential rent. Set aside a reserve for vacancy and maintenance, something real, not a rounding error, and you are still looking at your tenants covering the majority of that mortgage payment, often all of it, sometimes with room left over.

This is the same honest math the calculator on our Properties page runs live, with your actual numbers instead of this example. Depreciation and appreciation matter too over a longer hold, but the first thing worth understanding is simply this, your mortgage payment and your potential rental income are closer to each other than most people assume before they run the real numbers.

The comparison

Renting vs house hacking, the honest five year version

Renting a typical apartment in San Antonio runs somewhere around $1,300 to $1,500 a month for most veterans in this program. Over five years that is upward of $80,000 spent with nothing to show for it at the end, no equity, no asset, no tax benefit.

House hacking the same budget into a multifamily property means that money is still going out each month, but a meaningful share of it is building equity in something you own, paying down a loan balance that becomes yours, and sitting on a property that historically appreciates over time in this market.

The honest caveat, because we are not selling a fantasy here: owning comes with real responsibilities renting does not, repairs, tenant management, and property taxes among them. The comparison is not rent equals bad and own equals good, it is that the same monthly number can either disappear completely or partially build something, and most people have never had that second option explained to them clearly.

The next move

What happens after year one

A VA loan requires you to occupy the property, but it does not require you to stay forever. Most people house hack their first property for at least a year, since that occupancy period is part of the loan agreement, then have real options once that year is up.

You can keep living there and keep riding the strategy as is. You can move out and rent your own unit too, turning the property into a fully rental asset while you go live somewhere else. Or, if you still have remaining entitlement, you can potentially do this again with a second property, repeating the process.

This is the part that turns one smart purchase into an actual long term plan instead of a single event, and it is exactly the kind of thing worth walking through on a call with your specific numbers rather than guessing from a general article.
